Chapter 2: Nationalism in India

Class 10 History Notes

I. First World War, Khilafat & Non-Cooperation

Satyagraha: A novel method of mass agitation based on truth and non-violence, emphasizing the power of appealing to the oppressor's conscience rather than using physical force[cite: 14].
  • Wartime Hardships: Increased defense expenditure, high taxes, soaring prices, forced rural recruitment, crop failures, and an influenza epidemic created extreme distress[cite: 14].
  • Rowlatt Act (1919): Allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years, sparking nationwide protests and the Jallianwalla Bagh massacre (13 April 1919)[cite: 14].
  • Non-Cooperation Movement: Launched in support of Khilafat and Swaraj, involving the boycott of foreign goods, government schools, and legislative councils[cite: 14].

II. Differing Strands & Civil Disobedience Movement

Movements & Campaigns Key Developments
Countrysides & Plantations Awadh peasants struggled against high rents under Baba Ramchandra; tribal peasants in Gudem Hills engaged in guerrilla warfare under Alluri Sitaram Raju; plantation workers in Assam defied the Inland Emigration Act[cite: 14].
Civil Disobedience (1930) Initiated with Mahatma Gandhi's Salt March from Sabarmati to Dandi (breaking the salt law on 6 April 1930); participation of rich peasants, business classes, and women[cite: 14].
Limits & Pacts Dalit participation was limited; Dr B.R. Ambedkar established the Depressed Classes Association, leading to the Poona Pact (September 1932) after Gandhi's fast[cite: 14].

III. The Sense of Collective Belonging

Nationalism captured popular imagination through cultural processes: the creation of the Bharat Mata image by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and Abanindranath Tagore, the revival of Indian folklore, the reinterpretation of glorious ancient history, and the adoption of nationalist symbols and flags (such as the Swaraj flag designed by Gandhi in 1921)[cite: 14].
