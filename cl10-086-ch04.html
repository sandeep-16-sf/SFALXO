Carbon and Its Compounds

Hyper-Detailed and Comprehensive Class 10 Science Notes

1. Introduction to Carbon

Carbon is one of the most important elements in chemistry. It is present in living organisms, fuels, food, medicines, plastics, fibres and countless other substances. The enormous number and variety of compounds formed by carbon make the study of carbon compounds a major part of organic chemistry.

Carbon at a glance: Symbol = C; atomic number = 6; electronic configuration = 2,4; valency = 4. Carbon belongs to Group 14 of the periodic table.

The outermost shell of carbon contains four electrons. Carbon does not normally form stable ions by simply losing or gaining four electrons. Instead, it achieves stability mainly by sharing electrons and forming covalent bonds.

2. Why Is Carbon So Versatile?

The extraordinary versatility of carbon is mainly due to two properties: tetravalency and catenation.

2.1 Tetravalency

Tetravalency: The ability of carbon to form four covalent bonds because it has four valence electrons.

Carbon has the electronic configuration 2,4. Losing four electrons or gaining four electrons would require a large amount of energy. Carbon therefore completes its octet by sharing electrons with other atoms.

2.2 Catenation

Catenation: The ability of an element to form bonds with atoms of the same element, producing chains, branches and rings.

Carbon shows exceptional catenation because the C-C bond is strong and stable. Carbon atoms can therefore join with one another to form very long chains, branched structures and cyclic structures.

2.3 Carbon Can Form Multiple Bonds

Carbon can form single, double and triple bonds with other carbon atoms.

Single bond:   C - C

Double bond:   C = C

Triple bond:   C ≡ C
Key idea: Tetravalency explains why carbon can form four bonds, while catenation explains why carbon can build enormous carbon skeletons. Together, these properties account for much of the diversity of carbon compounds.

3. Covalent Bond

Covalent bond: A chemical bond formed by the mutual sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between atoms.

Carbon compounds generally have covalent bonds. A covalent bond allows atoms to achieve a more stable electronic configuration without complete transfer of electrons.

4. Properties of Covalent Compounds

  • Generally low melting and boiling points: The forces between many covalent molecules are comparatively weak, so less energy is required to separate the molecules.
  • Poor electrical conductivity: Most covalent compounds do not contain free ions or freely moving charged particles.
  • Usually poor solubility in water: Many carbon compounds are insoluble or only slightly soluble in water, although compounds containing suitable polar groups can be soluble.
  • Solubility in organic solvents: Many carbon compounds dissolve in organic solvents.
Important: These are general properties. They should not be treated as absolute rules for every covalent compound.

8. Homologous Series

Homologous series: A group of organic compounds having the same functional group, the same general formula and similar chemical properties, in which successive members differ by a -CH₂- unit.

Characteristics

  1. All members have the same functional group.
  2. All members can be represented by the same general formula.
  3. They show similar chemical properties.
  4. Successive members differ by -CH₂-.
  5. The molecular mass of successive members differs by 14 u.
  6. Physical properties such as melting point and boiling point show gradual variation with increasing molecular size.

Example: Homologous Series of Alkanes

Methane       CH₄
Ethane        C₂H₆
Propane       C₃H₈
Butane        C₄H₁₀
Pentane       C₅H₁₂

Each successive member differs from the previous member by CH₂.

Relative molecular mass difference = 12 + (2 × 1) = 14 u

11. Chemical Properties of Carbon Compounds

11.1 Combustion

Carbon compounds generally burn in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water, heat and light.

Carbon compound + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + Energy

For example, methane burns in oxygen:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + Heat

Combustion is an exothermic reaction.

Complete and Incomplete Combustion

When sufficient oxygen is available, complete combustion generally produces carbon dioxide and water. When oxygen is insufficient, incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide and/or carbon particles (soot).

Safety point: Carbon monoxide is poisonous because it interferes with the blood's ability to transport oxygen.

11.2 Oxidation

Oxidation of a carbon compound can involve addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen. Ethanol can be oxidised to ethanoic acid.

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH

Oxidising agents such as alkaline potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) or acidified potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) can be used.

11.3 Addition Reaction

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing double or triple bonds can undergo addition reactions.

Ethene adds hydrogen in the presence of a nickel catalyst to form ethane.

CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃-CH₃
                     Ni

This process is called hydrogenation.

Hydrogenation of Vegetable Oils

Vegetable oils generally contain unsaturated compounds. Hydrogenation can add hydrogen across carbon-carbon double bonds in the presence of a catalyst, producing more saturated products. This principle is used in the industrial processing of some vegetable oils.

11.4 Substitution Reaction

Saturated hydrocarbons generally undergo substitution reactions. In a substitution reaction, one atom or group in a molecule is replaced by another atom or group.

For example, methane reacts with chlorine in the presence of sunlight:

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
             Sunlight

One hydrogen atom of methane is replaced by chlorine.

CH₄ → CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄

12. Ethanol

Molecular formula: C₂H₅OH

Ethanol is an alcohol containing the -OH functional group.

CH₃ - CH₂ - OH

It is a colourless, volatile, flammable liquid and is miscible with water in ordinary conditions.

12.1 Combustion of Ethanol

C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O + Heat

12.2 Oxidation of Ethanol

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH

Ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid using a suitable oxidising agent.

12.3 Reaction of Ethanol with Sodium

Ethanol reacts with sodium to form sodium ethoxide and hydrogen gas.

2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂

Effervescence is observed because hydrogen gas is released.

12.4 Dehydration of Ethanol

When ethanol is heated with concentrated sulphuric acid at about 443 K, it loses water and forms ethene.

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
             conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K
Dehydration: A reaction in which water is removed from a compound.

13. Ethanoic Acid

Molecular formula: CH₃COOH

Ethanoic acid is commonly known as acetic acid. It is the main acid present in vinegar.

   ||
CH₃-C-OH

Ethanoic acid is a weak acid. Pure ethanoic acid freezes at about 290 K and can form ice-like crystals in colder conditions; this pure form is called glacial acetic acid.

13.1 Reaction with Sodium

2CH₃COOH + 2Na → 2CH₃COONa + H₂

13.2 Reaction with Sodium Hydroxide

Ethanoic acid neutralises sodium hydroxide to form sodium ethanoate and water.

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O

13.3 Reaction with Sodium Carbonate

2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O

13.4 Reaction with Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate

CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

Carbon dioxide is released with brisk effervescence.

The gas can be tested using lime water:

Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

Lime water turns milky because insoluble calcium carbonate is formed.

Esterification: The reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol in the presence of concentrated sulphuric acid to form an ester and water.

Ethanoic acid reacts with ethanol to form ethyl ethanoate.

15. Saponification

Saponification: The alkaline hydrolysis of an ester to form an alcohol and the salt of a carboxylic acid.

For example:

CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH

The term saponification is especially associated with the alkaline hydrolysis of fats and oils during soap manufacture.

16. Soaps and Detergents

16.1 Soap

Soaps are generally sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.

CH₃-(CH₂)ₙ-COO⁻ Na⁺

Hydrocarbon chain        Ionic head
Hydrophobic part         Hydrophilic part

16.2 Hydrophobic Tail

The long hydrocarbon part does not interact well with water. It is attracted towards oils and grease.

16.3 Hydrophilic Head

The ionic end interacts strongly with water and remains oriented towards the water.

16.4 Cleansing Action of Soap

Soap cleans oily dirt because its molecules contain two chemically different parts: a water-repelling hydrocarbon tail and a water-attracting ionic head.

16.5 Micelle Formation

Micelle: A cluster of soap molecules formed in water in which the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails point inward towards the oil/grease while the hydrophilic ionic heads point outward towards water.
             O⁻  O⁻  O⁻
           /           \
         O⁻    GREASE    O⁻
          |               |
          |               |
         tail           tail
           \           /
             tail tail

      Simplified micelle around grease

When the cloth or surface is agitated, the grease-containing micelles are detached from the surface and remain suspended in water. Rinsing with water removes the dirt.

16.6 Soap in Hard Water

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. These ions react with soap molecules to form insoluble salts called scum.

Soap + Ca²⁺ / Mg²⁺ → Insoluble calcium/magnesium salts (scum)

This wastes soap and reduces its cleansing efficiency.

16.7 Detergents

Detergents are synthetic cleansing agents. They generally remain effective in hard water because their calcium and magnesium salts are much more soluble than the insoluble salts formed by ordinary soaps.

20. Common Confusions and Exam Traps

Carbon vs Hydrocarbon

Not every carbon compound is a hydrocarbon. A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen.

CH₄        → Hydrocarbon

C₂H₅OH     → Not a hydrocarbon
             because it also contains oxygen

Saturated vs Unsaturated

Saturated means that carbon-carbon bonds are only single bonds. It does not mean that the compound contains no hydrogen.

Saturated:    C - C

Unsaturated:  C = C

Unsaturated:  C ≡ C

Ethane vs Ethene vs Ethyne

Ethane:    CH₃-CH₃       → single bond

Ethene:    CH₂=CH₂       → double bond

Ethyne:    HC≡CH         → triple bond

Soap vs Detergent

Soap performs poorly in hard water because calcium and magnesium ions form insoluble scum. Detergents are generally more effective in hard water.

Ethanol vs Ethanoic Acid

Ethanol:

CH₃-CH₂-OH


Ethanoic acid:

    O
    ||
CH₃-C-OH

The functional groups are different, so their chemical properties and reactions are different.

Carbon

Catenation

Carbon forms strong C-C chains, branches and rings.

Hydrocarbons

Compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.

Alkanes

Saturated hydrocarbons; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

Alkenes
