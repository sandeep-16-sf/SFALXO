Chapter 1: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe

Class 10 History Notes

I. The French Revolution and the Idea of the Nation

Nation-State: A state in which the majority of citizens, and not only rulers, develop a sense of common identity and shared history forged through struggles[cite: 13].
  • French Revolution (1789): Transferred sovereignty from absolute monarchy to a body of French citizens, proclaiming that people would constitute the nation and shape its destiny[cite: 13].
  • Collective Identity Measures: Introduction of la patrie (fatherland) and le citoyen (citizen), a new tricolour flag, National Assembly, uniform administrative system, and abolition of internal customs duties[cite: 13].
  • Napoleonic Code (1804): Did away with birth-based privileges, established equality before the law, secured property rights, and simplified administrative divisions across Europe[cite: 13].

II. The Making of Nationalism & Liberal Ideology

Core Concepts Overview
Social Classes Landed aristocracy formed a dominant, united upper class, while the majority comprised peasantry and new industrial middle classes (businessmen, industrialists, professionals)[cite: 13].
Liberal Nationalism Stood for individual freedom, equality before the law, government by consent, end of autocracy, and freedom of markets (e.g., formation of the Zollverein customs union in 1834)[cite: 13].
Conservatism & 1815 Following Napoleon's defeat, the Treaty of Vienna restored monarchies, suppressed dissent, and established strict press censorship under conservative regimes[cite: 13].

III. Revolutions, Unification & Visualising the Nation

During 1830–1848, liberal-nationalist revolutions spread across Europe (Greek independence, Frankfurt Parliament). Italy was unified through Sardinia-Piedmont under Cavour, Garibaldi, and Victor Emmanuel II[cite: 13]. Germany was unified under Prussian leadership by Otto von Bismarck through three wars, culminating in the proclamation of German Emperor William I in January 1871[cite: 13]. Nations were personified through female allegories like Marianne in France and Germania in Germany[cite: 13].
