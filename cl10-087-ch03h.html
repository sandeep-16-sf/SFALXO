Chapter 3: The Making of a Global World

Class 10 History Notes

I. Pre-Modern World & The Nineteenth Century

Indentured Labour: A bonded labourer under contract to work for an employer for a specific amount of time to pay off passage to a new country[cite: 15].
  • Silk Routes & Food Travels: Vibrant pre-modern trade links connecting Asia, Europe, and Africa; global transfer of crops (potatoes, maize) and deadly diseases like smallpox[cite: 15].
  • Nineteenth-Century Economy: Characterized by three flows: trade in goods, migration of labour, and movement of capital[cite: 15]. Abolition of British Corn Laws expanded global food production and migration[cite: 15].
  • Colonial Impact & Rinderpest: European Scramble for Africa (Berlin Conference 1885); rinderpest (cattle plague) in the 1890s destroyed 90% of African cattle, forcing Africans into wage labour[cite: 15].

II. The Inter-War Economy & Great Depression

Economic Phases Key Characteristics
Wartime Transformations First World War as the first modern industrial war; transformed the US from an international debtor to an international creditor[cite: 15].
Mass Production & Consumption Pioneered by Henry Ford using assembly-line production for the T-Model car in the 1920s[cite: 15].
The Great Depression (1929) Caused by agricultural overproduction and withdrawal of US loans; led to catastrophic declines in production and employment worldwide, heavily affecting Indian peasants[cite: 15].

III. Rebuilding a World Economy: Post-War Era

The Bretton Woods Conference (July 1944) established the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank to ensure post-war economic stability[cite: 15]. Decolonization led to independent nations in Asia and Africa; developing countries demanded a New International Economic Order (NIEO) through the G-77[cite: 15]. From the 1970s, MNCs relocated production to low-wage Asian countries[cite: 15].
